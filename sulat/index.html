Type terrain in Baybayin, Kana, Jawi, Devanagari, Hanzi, Thai, Telugu, Burmese, Javanese, and punctuation. Complete a line, then press Play to move it through translation and biome changes. Animals move across connected land and water.

Movement starts when you press Play.

Type on a keyboard or tap the keys. Each key draws one terrain. Hover over a key, or hold it, to see its name.

Typing

Each key draws one terrain, in a glyph chosen for its shape from Baybayin, Kana, Jawi, Devanagari, Hanzi, Thai, Telugu, Burmese, Javanese, or punctuation. Space leaves a gap. Enter completes a line. Delete removes the last glyph, or brings back the last completed line. Play moves the terrain up one row every two seconds; Pause holds everything. The line you are typing stays editable.

Terrain contact

Terrain changes when it stays in contact with other terrain across steps. Water touching forest can become estuary. Rivers and lakes form marsh beside grass, and oases beside desert or dunes. Estuaries and coasts form mangrove beside forest. Snow beside mountains becomes glacier; cold terrain changes mountain into alpine. Water or snow cools lava into rock. Cold water changes reef into kelp.

Water and ice

Water moves one cell along its row every two steps; lakes stay still. A glacier left with water, a gap, or the row end beside it for three steps can calve: the tip becomes cold water and an iceberg drifts along the row, cooling the water under it until it meets land.

Tides, floods, and storms

These arrive on their own. Every 16 steps the tide covers sand and coast beside the sea for 8 steps, then uncovers them. A flood spreads from rivers and lakes over low ground for 3 steps, stands, and recedes after 8, leaving grass on dry ground and marsh on grassland and sand. Water that changes while it stands does not recede.

One storm at a time crosses three rows. Rain greens desert, cools lava, and can start a flood over a river or lake. A thunderstorm strikes one cell per step and burns forest to open ground. A blizzard needs cold terrain; it lays snow and freezes water. A sandstorm needs desert; it dries grassland into sand and dunes. A typhoon needs sea; it washes away coast and reef and can start a flood.

Earthquakes

Earthquakes are rare. One turns nearby mountain into rock, rock and badlands into cave, cave into rock, and volcanic ground into lava, breaks glaciers, and opens a gap in the row at its center.

Animals

Animals are words. Land animals follow connected land and swimmers follow connected water; gaps and lava break routes. Deer, foxes, boars, monkeys, and civets keep to forest and open ground, bears to cold forest, goats to rock, crabs to shores, hares to snow, camels to desert, fish to any water. Long names are drawn smaller.

Elephants, carabaos, tigers, and rhinoceroses are larger, slower, and appear one at a time; whales cross open sea and crocodiles hold rivers and estuaries. Elephants and rhinoceroses can open forest into grassland, and carabaos can turn grass into marsh. Tigers take deer, boars, and monkeys; foxes take hares; crocodiles take fish.

Fliers

A flying animal is a word in three parts whose first and last parts are wings: folded while it sits, open in flight, so its name can be read only in the air. Forest patches release birds. Dragonflies, butterflies, fireflies, bees, and mosquitoes rise from wet ground and flowers, and bats from caves.

Where they settle, they can change the ground. Butterflies and bees turn grass into flowers. Bats turn grass into forest. Fireflies turn swamp and marsh into mangrove. Mosquitoes turn marsh into swamp and grass into marsh; dragonflies turn swamp back into marsh and take mosquitoes in flight.

Marks

A dotted underline marks text rewritten by translation. A thin underline marks a change from terrain contact. A thick underline marks a change from an event or an animal.

Translations

Translation works on completed lines. A word can change into a word of the same meaning in another script. A word can be split where another word begins with the sound of one of its syllables. Loose glyphs whose sounds spell a word can join into it. These are the words it knows; the sounds are working romanizations.
